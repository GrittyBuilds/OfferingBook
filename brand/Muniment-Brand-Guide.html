Muniment
Brand guide · Stage 2 of 4 · August 2026

The system of record for private offerings.

Muniment replaces CapitalVault. This document defines the name, the mark, the color and type systems in light and dark, the rules for numbers and dates, and the voice — everything needed to apply the brand consistently across the interface, the printed reports, and the certificates.

01

The name

A muniment is a document held as evidence of title — proof of what a person owns and the means to defend it. The word comes from the Latin munire, to fortify. It is the exact category of thing this software holds.

Pronunciation

MYOO · nih · ment

Stress the first syllable. Rhymes with monument but begins with the "mew" of music.

The one-line explainer

Use this verbatim in the About screen and the report footer:

"Muniments of title: the documents by which ownership is proved."

Written usage

  • Muniment — initial capital, never all-caps in running text, never MUNIMENT outside the wordmark itself.
  • It takes no article. Write "open Muniment" and "recorded in Muniment" — never "the Muniment."
  • It is a proper noun, not a common one. Never "a muniment file" or "muniments" when you mean the software.
  • No suffix. It is not "Muniment App," "Muniment Pro," or "Muniment System." The product is Muniment.
  • Data files, exports, and storage keys use the lowercase slug muniment — for example muniment-backup-2026-08-18.json.
  • The short form Muni is acceptable in casual internal speech but never appears in the interface or in anything printed. In securities contexts "muni" already means a municipal bond.

Taglines

  • The system of record for private offerings.  — primary. Descriptive, states the category, safe on a client-facing cover page.
  • Proof of title, in order.  — short form for the sidebar footer or a favicon-sized context.
  • Every subscription, allotment, and dollar — on the record.  — long form for an About screen.
  • What is held, and by whom.  — the plainest option; works well set in serif under the mark.

02

The mark

Three vertical forms on a solid base. Read one way it is a battlement — the crenellated wall that gives munire its meaning. Read another it is the shoulder rhythm of an M, or a set of records standing on a common ledger line. The brass base is the constant: the foundation the record rests on.

Construction

The mark is drawn on a 64 unit grid. Bars are 12 units wide with 8-unit gaps. Outer bars run y 9→41; the center bar is exactly half their height, y 25→41. The base spans the full 52-unit width at y 45→55, separated from the bars by a 4-unit seam. Corner radius is 1.5 units, dropped entirely below 20 px where it muddies the silhouette.

Rules

  • Keep clear space equal to one bar width (12/64 of the mark's height) on every side.
  • Below 20 px use the square-cornered favicon variant; below 16 px use the mark alone, never the lockup.
  • On brand green or any dark ground, bars go white and the base stays brass. Never reverse the base to white.
  • In one-color contexts the entire mark takes a single ink. Brass is the first thing to go, not the last.
  • Never rotate, skew, outline, or add a gradient, bevel, drop shadow, or glow.
  • Never change the bar proportions to fit a space — scale the whole mark instead.
  • Never recolor the bars to a semantic color (success green, danger red) to indicate state.
  • Never set the wordmark in the UI sans. The wordmark is always the serif, always tracked at 0.14em, always uppercase.
  • Never place the mark on a photograph or a patterned ground.

05

Numbers, money, and dates

This is a ledger before it is anything else. These rules are not stylistic preferences — misaligned or ambiguous figures in an escrow report are a liability, and a date read wrong at a closing is a real problem.

  • Every figure uses tabular lining numerals. Set font-variant-numeric: tabular-nums globally so columns align on the decimal without exception.
  • Money is right-aligned, always to two decimal places, even at whole dollars. $50,000.00, never $50,000. A dropped cent column is how reconciliation errors hide.
  • Negative amounts take a minus sign and the danger color — −$1,250.00. Never parentheses alone, never color alone; the two together survive both grayscale printing and color blindness.
  • Zero is $0.00, not a dash or an em dash. A dash means "not applicable"; zero means the balance is zero. They are different facts.
  • Dates in the interface are unambiguous: 14 Aug 2026. Never 08/14/26 anywhere a client might read it.
  • Dates in printed reports spell the month: 14 August 2026. Every report carries an "as of" date and a generation timestamp in its footer.
  • Percentages of ownership carry four decimals — 12.5000% — because they are computed from unit counts and rounding compounds across a class.
  • Identifiers are monospaced and never wrap — certificate numbers, subscription IDs, wire references.

06

Voice

Precise, unhurried, and never chatty. Muniment is a record-keeper, and a record-keeper does not celebrate. It states what happened, what is true now, and what is required next.

Do

"Escrow balance $4,275,000.00. Minimum raise met 12 Aug 2026."

State the fact and the date. Let the number carry the weight.

Don't

"Great news — you've hit your minimum raise! 🎉"

No exclamation points, no emoji, no congratulation. A closing is a legal event, not an achievement unlocked.

Rules

  • Never an exclamation point. Not in a toast, not in an empty state, not anywhere.
  • No emoji in the product. The padlock in the current title bar goes; icons are drawn, not typed.
  • Empty states describe the condition, then offer the action — "No closings recorded. Record a closing." Not "Nothing here yet!"
  • Destructive confirmations name the object and the consequence — "Delete closing 12 Aug 2026? This reverses 14 escrow entries and voids 9 certificates."
  • Errors say what failed and what to do, never "Oops" or "Something went wrong."
  • Use the profession's words: subscription, allotment, escrow, closing, certificate, exemption. Never "deal," "raise" as a noun in client-facing text, or "signup."
